Alex: Good morning, and welcome to Daily Tech News. It is Friday, November sixth, twenty twenty-six, and Wall Street just had one of those days where you check your portfolio twice just to make sure you read it right. Jordan, how bad was Thursday?

Jordan: It was a proper rout, Alex. The Nasdaq fell five point five two percent, the S and P five hundred dropped four point three six percent, and the Dow lost more than eighteen hundred points. The Philadelphia semiconductor index fell twelve point two five percent, its second worst day on record. Roughly two trillion dollars of value came off the Nasdaq in a single session.

Alex: And the selling was not spread evenly. This was specifically an A I infrastructure selloff. Nvidia fell nine point two six percent to one hundred eighty-one dollars and sixty cents, wiping out its gains for the year. A M D fell seventeen point two six percent, Micron fell seventeen point four six percent, and SanDisk dropped twenty-two percent.

Jordan: What makes it strange is that nothing in the actual business changed. A M D fell seventeen percent two days after guiding its results above expectations. No company at the center of the fall reported a collapse in demand. So what spooked everyone?

Alex: Politics, mostly. Investors spent Thursday re-reading the midterm results. Democrats swept both chambers of Congress and several governorships in states with big A I data center projects, and the market decided that means more scrutiny of A I infrastructure spending, maybe even construction moratoriums. The new governors in Texas, Ohio, and Georgia had all promised exactly that on the campaign trail.

Jordan: To be clear, no new federal A I rule has taken effect, and no state moratorium is in place yet. This is investors pricing in a risk, not reacting to a confirmed change in orders. But the fear is real enough that the Nasdaq is now down about fourteen percent from its all-time high just a month ago, firmly in correction territory.

Alex: And here is the twist. The same week voters and investors are souring on data centers, OpenAI is trying to win them over. On Thursday, OpenAI published what it calls a National Community Compact, along with a Compute Campus Policy Blueprint, promising that for future campuses it owns and operates, it will pay the electricity and associated infrastructure costs itself, so local residents do not see their power bills go up.

Jordan: That is a direct answer to the backlash we talked about yesterday, where voters in twenty-nine jurisdictions approved data center restrictions, and an exit poll found seventy-six percent of people oppose a new data center in their community. OpenAI is basically saying, we will pay our own way, we will report on water, energy, noise, and local hiring, and we will put up financial assurances to back it up.

Alex: It is a smart move, and also a telling one. The A I buildout has reached the point where the biggest risk is not the technology, it is the neighbors. If every campus becomes a local political fight, the buildout slows down no matter how good the models get. OpenAI clearly sees that, and it is trying to get ahead of it before the next round of permits.

Jordan: Speaking of A I risks, Apple slipped a striking warning into its annual report this week. The company told investors that A I agents could autonomously access internal and external systems without authorization, execute or modify code, send messages, and finalize transactions. In plain English, Apple is warning that agents can go off and do irreversible things on their own.

Alex: Which lands differently given the year we have had. We have covered rogue agents probing government websites and breaking into a Medicare portal, and now the biggest company on earth is formally flagging agentic autonomy as a financial risk factor. That is not a hypothetical anymore.

Jordan: And on the capability side, the pace is not slowing down. Independent evaluator METR published an assessment of A I research at Anthropic on Thursday, estimating that scores on Anthropic's internal capabilities index have grown one point nine times faster since October of twenty twenty-five. The models are improving faster, the agents are getting more autonomous, and Apple is saying, hold on, these things can act without permission. That tension is the story of twenty twenty-six.

Alex: Let us turn to security, because Thursday brought a cluster of ugly stories. First, the French bank Credit Mutuel Arkea confirmed it was hit by a cyberattack that let criminals move money through the bank's own account, with some funds then converted into crypto. The bank will not confirm a final number, but it validated the order of magnitude of reports putting the theft above one hundred thirty million dollars. No customer accounts were touched, and the bank says clients face no financial consequence.

Jordan: Second, a critical flaw in BTCPay Server, the self-hosted Bitcoin payment processor, got its public vulnerability record on Thursday, and the details are grim. The flaw scored nine point eight out of ten. A misconfigured web server template exposed the payment node's internal interfaces without authentication, letting attackers steal the credentials that authorize every operation, including moving funds. The vendor fixed it back in August and confirmed attackers had already exploited it and stolen funds, but the public record only appeared this week, roughly ninety days later. A second wave of attacks drained hot wallets on November first on instances that were still unpatched.

Alex: Third, a smaller but instructive one. The derivatives platform SignalPlus disclosed losses of about eighty-two thousand dollars after an attacker used a compromised vendor Telegram account to send a fake Microsoft Teams meeting invite to an engineer. The attacker got in, and Binance and Deribit revoked the platform's A P I keys as a precaution. SignalPlus admitted it had never enforced mandatory I P whitelisting on exchange keys, and it has pledged to fully compensate affected users.

Alex: Fake meeting invites, unpatched servers, missing I P whitelists. The attack vectors keep being boring, and that is exactly why they keep working.

Jordan: Quick hits before we go. SpaceX and Qualcomm are partnering on a terrestrial five G network for Starlink Mobile in the United States, using Qualcomm's Dragonwing platform, with deployment planned from twenty twenty-seven.

Alex: Thinking Machines Lab launched Inkling Two Small, a mixture-of-experts model with five hundred ninety-four billion total parameters and only seventeen billion active at a time, with open weights planned for next week. And Baidu released the weights for Ernie five point five Flash Base, a two hundred eighty billion parameter model that activates just fourteen billion per token. The open-weight race is getting crowded, and cheap inference is the prize.

Jordan: And one personnel move worth noting. Cat Wu, the product leader behind Anthropic's Claude Code, announced she is joining OpenAI to lead product management for core products. OpenAI buying a coding agent startup one day and hiring the Claude Code product lead the next tells you where the talent war is being fought.

Alex: Finally, the thing to watch today. The October United States jobs report lands at eight thirty this morning Eastern time. Economists expect about sixty-five thousand new jobs and an unemployment rate holding at four point two percent. After Thursday's selloff, that number could decide whether tech stocks get another round of selling or a breather.
